# IxD study digest for Amber

**How I verified.** Web research only; no files written. Each URL was opened, or confirmed in a search result where the publisher blocked my fetcher ("blocked"). Tags: **[Study]** measured data; **[Opinion]** practitioner judgement; **[Convention]** a standard or vendor rule; **[Secondary]** I saw only a summary. Read first: Norman, MIT 6.813 "Safety", Nielsen, Shneiderman, Sellen et al., Few, Ware/Healey, Victor, WCAG, High-Performance HMI.

## 1. Sources

### Definitions and frameworks
1. **Designing Interactions**, Bill Moggridge, MIT Press, 2006 (often cited 2007). https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262134743/designing-interactions/ (blocked; quote at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interaction_design). Why: he called it "Soft-face" in 1984, then settled on "interaction design" "with the help of Bill Verplank" (p. 14), so credit is shared. Take: design behaviour for people's needs and lasting satisfaction, not just looks.
2. **Interaction Design Sketchbook**, Bill Verplank, c. 2001. https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~verplank/S2S/InteractionDesignSketchbook.PDF. Why: Do/Feel/Know. How do you *do* (button = one-off command, handle = continuous control), *feel* (what comes back to your senses), *know* (a map or path telling you where you are)? Take: ask all three of every control.
3. **"What Puts the Design in Interaction Design"**, Kevin Silver, UXmatters, 2007. https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2007/07/what-puts-the-design-in-interaction-design.php. Why: the "five dimensions" are Gillian Crampton Smith's four (words, visuals, physical objects/space, time) plus Silver's fifth, behaviour. Take: timing and reactions are design material.
4. **About Face 4**, Cooper, Reimann, Cronin, Noessel, Wiley, 2014. https://www.wiley.com/en-us/About+Face%3A+The+Essentials+of+Interaction+Design%2C+4th+Edition-p-9781118766576 (contents: https://books.google.com/books/about/About_Face.html?id=4c4XBAAAQBAJ). Why: goal-directed design (design for what people are trying to achieve), personas (research-based archetypes), posture (attention a product demands: sovereign = long, full-screen; transient = brief; daemonic = background). Take: design for the "perpetual intermediate", neither novice nor expert.
5. **Designing for Interaction** (2nd ed., 2010) and **Microinteractions** (2013), Dan Saffer. https://searchworks.stanford.edu/view/13188678 ; https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/microinteractions/9781449342760/. Why: a microinteraction (one small task, like a toggle) = trigger, rules, feedback, loops, modes; the book covers spring-loaded modes. Take: spec every control as those five parts.
6. **Designing Interfaces**, 3rd ed., Tidwell, Brewer, Valencia, O'Reilly, 2020. https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/designing-interfaces-3rd/9781492051954/ (blocked). Why: catalogue of UI patterns (proven reusable solutions). Take: look for a pattern before inventing one.
7. **The Humane Interface**, Jef Raskin, Addison-Wesley, 2000. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Humane_Interface (summary). Why: a *mode* is a state where the same input does different things; a *quasimode* lasts only while you hold something (like Shift); *habituation* = repeated prompts become automatic; *monotony* = one way per task. Take: avoid modes or make them held; "Are you sure?" gets clicked through.

### Cognitive foundations
8. **The Design of Everyday Things**, revised ed., Don Norman, Basic Books, 2013. https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/don-norman/the-design-of-everyday-things/9780465050659/. Why: signifiers (visible cues about what to do), mappings (control layout matching the thing controlled), feedback, constraints, conceptual models (the user's picture of how it works), gulfs, slips and mistakes. Take: "human error" is usually bad design.
9. **"Direct Manipulation Interfaces"**, Hutchins, Hollan, Norman, 1985. https://vis.csail.mit.edu/classes/6.859/readings/pdfs/Hutchins-DirectManipulationInterfaces.pdf. Why: develops the gulfs of execution/evaluation and defines "directness". Take: judge a control by the distance between intention and action.
10. **Eight Golden Rules**, Ben Shneiderman, *Designing the User Interface* 6th ed., 2016. https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/ben/goldenrules.html. Why: consistency, universal usability, informative feedback, closure, error prevention, easy reversal, user control, low memory load; "derived from experience", needing "validation and tuning" (his words). Direct manipulation (1983): visible objects, rapid, reversible, incremental actions. Take: a review checklist.
11. **Designing with the Mind in Mind**, 3rd ed., Jeff Johnson, Morgan Kaufmann, 2020. https://shop.elsevier.com/books/designing-with-the-mind-in-mind/johnson/978-0-12-818202-4. Why: chapters such as "Our peripheral vision is poor", "Our color vision is limited". Take: knowing why guidelines work lets you trade them off.
12. **Don't Make Me Think, Revisited**, Steve Krug, New Riders, 2014. https://sensible.com/dont-make-me-think/. Why: practitioner classic. Take: make things self-evident; people scan and muddle through; test early with a few people.
13. **Keystroke-Level Model**, Card, Moran, Newell, CACM 1980 / *Psychology of HCI* 1983. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystroke-level_model (summary). Why: GOMS breaks a task into goals, operators, methods and selection rules; KLM prices steps: keystroke 0.2 s, point 1.1 s, hand-move 0.4 s, mental pause 1.35 s (experts, keyboard and mouse). Take: count steps to compare designs.
14. **Fitts's law**, Paul Fitts 1954; I. S. MacKenzie, "Fitts' law as a research and design tool in HCI", 1992. https://www.yorku.ca/mack/hci1992.pdf (author: https://www.yorku.ca/mack/). Why: pointing time grows with distance divided by target width (MacKenzie: a + b·log2(D/W+1)). Take: bigger, closer targets are faster and missed less.
15. **Hick–Hyman law**, W. E. Hick 1952, Ray Hyman 1953. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hick%27s_law. Why: choice time is about b·log2(n+1) for equally likely options; weak for familiar or practised choices. Take: group controls rather than hide them.
16. **Gestalt principles** (early 1900s); summary https://www.nngroup.com/articles/gestalt-proximity/ and Johnson ch. 2-3. Why: things near each other, in one region, or joined by a line read as related. Take: put each button beside its own vessel's readout.
17. **Laws of UX**, Jon Yablonski, 2020 (O'Reilly 2nd ed. 2024). https://lawsofux.com/ ; https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/laws-of-ux/9781098146955/. Why: one-page digests (Fitts, Hick, Miller, Doherty <400 ms, Jakob's Law = people expect your product to work like others). Take: the site calls them "best practices"; strength varies, so read the source.

### Heuristics and courses
18. **10 Usability Heuristics**, Jakob Nielsen, 1994, reviewed 2024. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/. Why: derived by factor analysis of real usability problems (CHI '94: https://dlnext.acm.org/doi/epdf/10.1145/191666.191729). **Response times**: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/. Take: 0.1 s feels instant, 1 s keeps your train of thought, 10 s is the attention limit (from Miller 1968, Card et al. 1991).
19. **MIT 6.813/6.831**, Rob Miller. https://web.mit.edu/6.813/www/sp18/classes/04-safety/ (2018 "Safety" reading); OCW 2011 notes (usability, learnability, visibility, efficiency, errors): https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-831-user-interface-design-and-implementation-spring-2011/pages/lecture-notes/. Why: best short treatment of slips, mode errors and spring-loaded modes. Take: undo beats confirmation dialogs; keep dangerous commands away from common ones.
20. **CS 6750**, David Joyner, Georgia Tech. https://omscs.gatech.edu/cs-6750-human-computer-interaction (public lessons via Ed Lessons; old Udacity link now redirects to a paid course). Why: principles plus needfinding, prototype, evaluate. Take: find needs before drawing.
21. **CS 147**, Stanford. https://cs147.stanford.edu/ (Autumn 2026 page). Why: design-thinking course. Take: prototype early; evaluate from critique to usability tests.
22. **Interaction Design: Beyond Human-Computer Interaction**, 6th ed., Rogers, Sharp, Preece, Wiley, 2023. https://www.id-book.com/ ; https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Interaction+Design%3A+Beyond+Human+Computer+Interaction%2C+6th+Edition-p-9781119901099. Why: the standard textbook (author order varies by edition). Take: a broad reference for usability goals and methods.
23. **IxDF literature**, Interaction Design Foundation. https://www.interaction-design.org/literature. Why: about 1,770 free articles from a training company. Take: quality varies; use as signposts to primary sources.

### Platform guidance (living documents, no fixed year)
24. **Apple HIG**. https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons : hit area at least 44×44 pt; give custom buttons a press state; don't rely on one method to convey information.
25. **Material Design 3**. https://m3.material.io/ ; Google's note https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/7101858 : 48×48 dp (about 9 mm), 8 dp apart.
26. **Microsoft Fluent 2**. https://fluent2.microsoft.design/ ; https://fluent2.microsoft.design/accessibility : AA contrast, 4.5:1 text, 3:1 large text and non-text.
27. **GOV.UK Government Design Principles** (2012, updated 2025). https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles : start with user needs, do the hard work to make it simple, iterate, understand context, be consistent not uniform.

### Displays of information
28. **Magic Ink**, Bret Victor, 2006. https://worrydream.com/MagicInk/. Why: "information software" (for learning) vs "manipulation software" (for creating); interactivity is a "last resort". Take: make the default view answer questions without touch.
29. **The Visual Display of Quantitative Information**, Edward Tufte, 1983/2001. https://www.edwardtufte.com/book/the-visual-display-of-quantitative-information/ ; *Beautiful Evidence*, 2006 (sparklines: word-sized trend lines) https://www.edwardtufte.com/book/beautiful-evidence/. Why: data-ink ratio (ink that shows data, not decoration), small multiples (repeated mini-charts in one format). Take: identical panels per vessel; sparkline trends.
30. **Information Dashboard Design**, 2nd ed., Stephen Few, Analytics Press, 2013. https://www.perceptualedge.com/library.php ; bullet graph spec https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/misc/Bullet_Graph_Design_Spec.pdf. Why: dashboards for rapid monitoring; a bullet graph shows a value against a target and quality bands in one compact bar. Take: show target and range beside the number.
31. **Information Visualization: Perception for Design**, 4th ed., Colin Ware, 2019. https://shop.elsevier.com/books/information-visualization/ware/978-0-12-812875-6 ; summary of preattentive processing (features noticed in about 200-250 ms): https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/healey/PP/. Take: one salient cue at a time.

## 2. Principles

1. **Ask Do/Feel/Know of every control.** Can I act on it, what comes back, what must I understand? Verplank [Opinion].
2. **Design for goals, and for the perpetual intermediate.** Goal: "hit mash temperature, know what's next." Monitor = long-attention ("sovereign") screen; phone = quick check-in (my reading). Cooper [Opinion, case-based].
3. **Close both gulfs.** Gulf of execution: the gap between what I want and what the controls let me do. Gulf of evaluation: the gap between what the system shows and what I can tell about its state. Norman; Hutchins et al. [Theory, widely applied].
4. **Make controls look operable (signifiers), mirror the plant (natural mapping), make wrong moves hard (constraints).** Norman's 2013 edition reserves "affordance" for what is possible, "signifier" for the cue. Norman; Nielsen #2 [Opinion grounded in cognitive science].
5. **Expect slips more than mistakes.** A slip is right goal, wrong action; a mistake is a wrong plan. MIT notes put slips at roughly 1-5% per step of a learned routine (context-dependent). Fix slips with spacing, dissimilarity and constraints: keep dangerous commands away from frequent ones; avoid rows of look-alike buttons. Norman; Reason; MIT [Mixed].
6. **Remove modes; if you can't, make them held.** A held state (quasimode, "spring-loaded" mode) can't be forgotten. In a lab text-editing experiment, user-held mode switches prevented more errors than a visual mode indicator; not tested on heaters. Raskin; MIT; Sellen et al. [Study + Opinion].
7. **Undo beats "Are you sure?"; where undo is impossible (heat), add friction to starting and none to stopping (my synthesis).** Repeated confirmations become habit and get clicked through. ⚠ Sources differ: Raskin would drop warnings, Shneiderman allows a few, MIT says only for hard-to-reverse actions. Hold-to-run is an established machine-safety control (IEC 60204-1) [Opinion + Convention, secondary].
8. **Answer at the point of touch, at the speed of touch.** About 0.1 s feels instant, 1 s keeps flow, 10 s loses attention. Peripheral vision is poor, so feedback away from the finger is missed; MIT: mode status must sit in the user's locus of attention (the Caps Lock light "doesn't really work"); Apple says always give a press state. ⚠ Doherty's 400 ms measures productivity, not "feels instant". Nielsen; Johnson; MIT [rules of thumb from perception research].
9. **Show requested, actual and fault separately, and show when data is stale.** "Pump commanded on" is not "pump moving liquid"; a frozen number must not look alive. Nielsen #1; Norman [Opinion].
10. **Support three levels of awareness: perceive, understand, project.** For a vessel: the value, the gap to target, the trend or time-to-target. Endsley [Study/theory, mostly aviation and control rooms].
11. **Targets: bigger and nearer is faster and safer; published minimums differ.** Apple 44 pt; Google 48 dp (about 9 mm, 8 dp apart); NN/g about 1 cm (from a touch study; fingertips are 1.6-2 cm wide); WCAG 2.2 AA 24 CSS px, AAA 44. All assume dry fingers at arm's length; wet hands and distance justify larger (my inference). Water drops can cause false touches on some capacitive sensors (NXP). Fitts; MacKenzie [Study + Convention].
12. **Size text by visual angle, not points.** Character height at least 16 arcminutes, 20-22 preferred (NUREG-0700, secondary). Height is about distance × 0.0047 (minimum) or × 0.0064 (preferred): at 3 m, 14 / 19 mm; at 5 m, 23 / 32 mm [Convention].
13. **Choice time grows with the number of options, only logarithmically, and not for practised choices.** So don't hide critical controls to "reduce choices"; group them and keep positions fixed so habits form. Laws of UX's "minimize choices" is looser than the law. Hick; Hyman [Study, narrow scope].
14. **Group by proximity, common region and connection; proximity can override colour or shape.** One vessel, one group, buttons beside its readouts, plumbing lines showing flow. Gestalt; NN/g; Johnson [Study + practice].
15. **Pop-out works only when one thing differs.** Preattentive features (colour, size, motion, orientation) are spotted in about 200-250 ms; if many things are red or moving, none stands out. Ware; Healey [Study].
16. **Colour is scarce, and never the only cue.** Add shape, text, position (WCAG 1.4.1; Apple); red-green confusion is the commonest deficiency (NEI). ⚠ Three colour vocabularies collide here: PID faceplate (red PV, green SV), industrial lamp code (red emergency, amber abnormal, green normal; secondary), heat (red = hot). Jakob's Law favours the PID convention the owner knows; if kept, separate PV from SV by size, position and label, and signal alarms with more than red [Convention + Opinion].
17. **High-performance HMI: muted normal state, colour only for abnormal, graphical range bars.** Evidence is one small industry-led field study: 21 operators; 96% vs 70% handled the incident with the ASM-style interface (different plant units). ISA-101 is a lifecycle standard and, per a secondary source, does not dictate colours. Hollifield; Errington [Opinion + weak Study].
18. **Dashboards: one screen, numbers with context (target, range, trend).** Bullet graphs, sparklines and small multiples make deviations visible by pattern. ⚠ Tufte's data-ink minimalism is opinion; Bateman et al. found decorated charts read as accurately and were remembered better after two weeks (static charts, not monitoring). Few; Tufte [Opinion informed by perception science].
19. **Treat the screen mostly as information software.** Show what matters automatically (active step, next action, vessel needing attention); touch is for commands. ⚠ Victor calls interactivity a last resort; Shneiderman and Hutchins et al. defend direct manipulation for the manipulation parts. Victor [Opinion].
20. **Alarms must require a response, and be rare.** EEMUA 191 benchmark: about 1 per operator per 10 minutes; ISA-18.2 defines an alarm as needing a timely response (both secondary). Habituation explains why constant alarms get ignored. Raskin [Convention + Opinion].
21. **Don't lean on memory: recognition over recall, about four chunks.** ⚠ Shneiderman and Laws of UX quote 7 plus or minus 2; Cowan (2001) says about 4, and Miller's 7 was "meant more as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device". Nielsen #6 [Study].
22. **Consistency helps learning; near-duplicates hurt safety.** ⚠ Raskin wants one way per task; Nielsen #7 and Shneiderman want shortcuts for experts; for commands here, one obvious path is safer (my inference). MIT; GOV.UK "consistent, not uniform" [Opinion].
23. **Count steps to compare designs.** KLM: a tap on a visible Stop is about mental pause 1.35 + point 1.1 + press 0.2 = 2.65 s; each extra screen adds about that again. Rough for touch. Card, Moran, Newell [Study, limited].
24. **Test in context, small and often.** GOV.UK ("understand context", "iterate"), Krug, CS 147, Joyner. A heuristic review is a cheap first pass, not a substitute for watching the owner brew [Opinion, well supported in practice].

## 3. Applied checklist

**Before choosing a layout**
1. What must be answerable in one glance from 3-5 m with no touch? (Is anything wrong? Which vessel is off target, heading which way? What's next, and when? What's running?)
2. Which tasks are urgent, frequent or irreversible (stop heat, silence alarm, pause/advance step, start heat, pumps)? Have you counted steps for each?
3. Does on-screen order follow the physical brewery and the plumbing path?
4. Is each vessel one visual group, with buttons beside their own readouts?
5. What does each colour mean here, one meaning each? What is the single "needs a human now" cue, and does anything else use it? Is the screen readable in greyscale?
6. For each actuator, which states exist (off, requested, running, confirmed, fault, unknown/stale), and can each be told apart without colour?
7. Which modes exist (AUTO/MANUAL, heat enabled, edit/run, step paused)? Can any go, or become held? Is each visible at the control it changes?
8. What can the screen not prevent (dry-firing, runaway)? Are firmware interlocks in place so the UI isn't the last defence? (Norman's forcing functions; my inference.)
9. What is the monitor for and what is the phone for? On the phone: check, acknowledge, stop?
10. Have you watched the owner brew in the garage to see what they look at, miss and touch?

**Before hand-off**
1. Does every command answer at its own button: press state under 0.1 s, "sent" within about 1 s, then confirmation of the real effect, with a visible failure if the controller doesn't reply?
2. Held press: progress shows on the button; lifting early or sliding off cancels cleanly (WCAG 2.5.2 idea); stop is a plain tap; tested wet and gloved, with a droplet on the glass.
3. Targets at least the largest published minimum (about 1 cm / 48 dp / 44 pt), larger for primary controls, with gaps; dangerous controls away from frequent ones; no look-alike rows.
4. Text height meets the visual angle at the real distance; contrast at least 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for large text, icons and borders; checked in glare, in the dark, and with a colour-blindness simulation.
5. Each alarm has a cause, priority, required action and acknowledge/snooze; none exist for pure information; audible over pumps; counted in a busy mash-in.
6. The three vessel panels behave identically; look-alike labels and icons are distinguishable.
7. Do error messages say what happened and what to do? Is "all heat off" one tap from anywhere?
8. Does anything make the user remember a value across screens? Cut it.
9. Timed test with the owner: silence alarm, stop heat, find next step, from 3 m with wet hands. Log slips; fix one thing per round; run the Nielsen ten as a cheap second pass.

**Settings redesign**
1. Rarely used, so use standard patterns: labelled fields with units and current values, grouped by task, not by hardware.
2. Are dangerous settings (PID tuning, power limits, sensor assignment, calibration) separated and guarded by effort and undo, not dialogs?
3. Does it say what applies now, on save or next brew, and whether a running brew is affected?
4. Can every change be reset or undone, and are errors specific?
5. Does the phone layout reflow without losing information?
